DEPT v19 Review Resumes: Lockdep Integration and PG_lock Semantics

DEPT v19 Review Resumes: Lockdep Integration and PG_lock Semantics

Classification

This remains a new kernel feature under review: Byungchul Park's 40-patch DEPT (DEPendency Tracker) series, posted as v19 on 6 July. This week's two messages do not introduce NFS protocol changes or NFS client bug fixes. They reopen the architectural questions that have shadowed the series since earlier revisions — whether DEPT should exist as a sibling of Lockdep, and how (or whether) folio PG_lock should be tracked at all.

Bridging Prior Weeks (Briefly)

v19 landed in the week ending 9 July with immediate scrutiny on two patches: Matthew Wilcox challenged per-page PG_locked tracking (patch 22/40), and Gary Guo flagged a Rust dept_key collision in wait_for_completion() (patch 31/40). The following week Park confirmed a Rust-side init_completion_dkey() plan and agreed to merge two Rust hygiene patches. Then the thread went quiet for five weeks. The only NFS-tree patch in the series remains the SUNRPC rcu_head relocation (patch 37/40, Reviewed-by Jeff Layton), needed because DEPT's debug instrumentation pushes offsetof(struct rpc_xprt, rcu) past kfree_rcu()'s 4096-byte limit.

This Week's Activity (14–20 August)

Two messages, both on 20 August, revived the cover letter rather than any individual patch.

1. David Hildenbrand questions the Lockdep split — and the folio example

David Hildenbrand (Arm) replied to the v19 cover letter after a long silence, with a tone that is more process-and-architecture than patch nits. He opened by noting that locking maintainers had already given "plenty of feedback" and that he might be restating old objections, then attacked Park's guiding deadlock example — the folio-lock / mutex interleaving Lockdep cannot see.

His reduction of that example is blunt:

"But that really just boils down to folio lock being implemented as a PG_lock + some advanced wait mechanism. And we must do that because of lack of bits in struct page."

He then quoted Wilcox's earlier (v17-era) objection that folio locking rules belong to mappings and anon_vmas, not to individual pages, and offered a Lockdep-shaped alternative instead of DEPT's new wait/event engine:

"Given that lockdep is a debug feature, and we will at some point allocate struct folio separately, I assume we could just squeeze a struct lockdep_map in there in such debug configs and the world would not collapse."

He conceded that stuffing a lockdep_map into every struct page today would break mm_zero_struct_page(), but sketched a CONFIG_LOCKDEP_PAGE_LOCK debug kernel as "probably an easier way to achieve it than some completely new infrastructure."

The second half of the mail goes after DEPT's independence. Park's FAQ had said Lockdep is "stable, battle-tested" and that retrofitting a wait/event model "would risk its stability." Hildenbrand does not accept that:

"Why can't this just be some configurable extension to lockdep (CONFIG_LOCKDEP_XYZ) until the feature is stable and can unconditionally be enabled along with it?"

"I don't quite buy the 'would risk its stability' argument. A lot of stuff we do 'risks stability', every day :)"

He also objects to the dual-mechanism endgame Park described — Lockdep remaining for lock-usage checks, DEPT eventually superseding only the dependency-checking logic:

"It's quite unfortunate that we'd end up with another similar-but-different mechanism, that will just end up confusing people."

The closer is the question the series has not answered in public at v19:

"Long story short: we are now in v19 and I think there was pushback in the past. Did the opinion of locking maintainers change, or is there a way forward to integrate this in a way that would make locking maintainers accept this?"

2. Wilcox restates why PG_lock is not a Lockdep-shaped lock

Thirty-five minutes later, Matthew Wilcox answered Hildenbrand's folio-lock suggestion by expanding the argument he first made in November 2025 (the mail Hildenbrand cited as [1]). He listed three properties that make PG_lock a poor fit for a per-object lockdep_map:

Lifetime vs. semantics. A struct page lives for the life of the system, but the meaning of its lock bit is reset on every free/alloc cycle:

"The current lifetime of a struct page is the lifetime of the system. But the semantics of its PG_lock bit change each time it is freed and allocated."

Hierarchy is an owner property, not a page property. All folios of a given XFS inode share locking rules; DEPT (or Lockdep) should classify by owner, and possibly even coarser than per-inode:

"The position of PG_lock in the locking hierarchy only depend on what the folio is currently being used for. That is, all folios in a given xfs inode behave exactly the same from a locking perspective. There's no need to build up state about how each PG_lock is used; they can all share."

He then pushed the classification one level further than "this folio belongs to this inode":

"Arguably all xfs file inodes are the same as each other (directory inodes might be different from file inodes), so we might want to go further than telling DEPT that 'this folio belongs to this inode' and go to 'this folio belongs to this xfs file inode'."

Task-context acquire, interrupt-context release — with an I/O-submit hole. This is the point most relevant to networked filesystems. PG_lock can be taken in process context and dropped from interrupt context once I/O completes. Tracking the lock bit alone is not enough; the detector must know when the folio was actually submitted:

"PG_lock can be taken in task context then released in interrupt context. For full points, we need to mark the exact point at which we submit the folio for read. Otherwise we can get into the situation alluded to by f2c817bed58d and better discussed at Jan Kara, Jan 2020 where we have the folio locked but haven't yet submitted it for I/O so it doesn't matter how long we wait, it will never come unlocked."

That last sentence is the classic "locked folio, I/O never started" hang — the pattern f2c817bed58d ("mm: don't allow unbounded locked-folio waits") was written to contain. A detector that treats PG_lock as an ordinary mutex will mis-report both that hang and the legitimate lock-in-process / unlock-in-IRQ path used by every filesystem's read and writeback completion.

Wilcox did not endorse Hildenbrand's CONFIG_LOCKDEP_PAGE_LOCK shortcut, nor did he bless DEPT's current per-page wgen design. He restated the owner-based classification he has been asking for since v17, and added the I/O-submit annotation as a correctness requirement.

Evolution of the Discussion

The week's arc is revival, not resolution:

  1. After five weeks of silence, Hildenbrand treats v19 as still lacking a locking-maintainer verdict and challenges both the folio motivating example and the "must be a separate subsystem" claim.
  2. Wilcox, whose earlier mapping/anon_vma comments Hildenbrand quoted, does not take the offered Lockdep-in-folio path. He instead explains why folio locks are a class of wait/event, not a class of lock objects, and why any detector — DEPT or Lockdep — must classify by owner and annotate I/O submit.

Park's replies to both mails land on 21 August, outside this window, so this week ends with Hildenbrand's questions unanswered on-list.

Significance for NFS Client Development

No NFS client patches moved this week. The discussion still matters for the client for three reasons:

  • Page-lock / writeback / readahead are NFS's daily PG_lock paths. NFS client I/O sleeps on folio locks, submits READ/WRITE RPCs, and unlocks from the RPC completion path (softirq / workqueue), which is exactly Wilcox's "task acquire, interrupt-or-async release" pattern. A detector that cannot mark the submit point will either scream about every NFS read or miss the "locked but never submitted" hang.
  • Owner-based classification is the NFS-shaped design. Wilcox's "all folios of this inode share locking rules" maps cleanly onto nfsi->vfs_inode.i_mapping. Until DEPT (or a Lockdep extension) tracks folio locks at the address_space / filesystem-type level rather than in struct page, reports against NFS writeback and readahead will mix false positives (stale wgen after reclaim) with false negatives (same-mapping lock order).
  • The SUNRPC rcu_head relocation is still the only in-tree NFS change, and it is a compile-time bug fix for CONFIG_DEPT builds, not a runtime NFS fix. Nothing this week revisits it.

Final Conclusions (as of window close)

  • The series is still v19, still experimental, still without a locking-maintainer Ack.
  • Hildenbrand's position: the folio example does not by itself justify a new subsystem; a debug lockdep_map in struct folio plus a CONFIG_LOCKDEP_* extension would be the conservative path; dual similar mechanisms will confuse users.
  • Wilcox's position: PG_lock is special because of page lifetime, owner-based hierarchy (possibly as coarse as "XFS file inode"), and lock-in-task / unlock-in-IRQ with an I/O-submit hole. That is an argument against treating folio lock as a normal Lockdep object, but it is also unfinished business for DEPT's current per-page wgen.
  • No merge path, no code change, no NFS client bug fix this week. The open design question is unchanged from July: classify folio waits by owner (and annotate I/O submit), and explain to locking maintainers why this cannot live under Lockdep.
