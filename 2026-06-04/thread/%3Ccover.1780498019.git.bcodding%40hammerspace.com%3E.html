nfsd per-client fair-queue dispatch RFC: identity-based fairness reframed into a sparse-flow service floor

Summary

nfsd per-client fair-queue dispatch: an RFC that quickly outgrew its own design

What landed this week

Benjamin Coddington posted a four-patch RFC on June 3 proposing per-client fair-queue dispatch for nfsd, targeting a long-standing starvation problem: because the server pulls ready transports from a single per-pool FIFO, "a client's share of nfsd service scales with the number of connections it holds rather than being shared per client."

The cover letter is unusually data-driven. Coddington measured an 8-thread pool doing fixed-latency operations (10 ms/op, ~648 ops/s ceiling) and showed that a single-connection interactive client drops from 241 ops/s down to 38 as a greedy peer opens 1→16 connections:

"The interactive client's share tracks 1/(K+1) and its throughput falls roughly 6x while it does nothing different. NFSv4.1 behaves identically (89% greedy at K=8) even when the greedy connections are bound to a single session, because the dispatch decision is below the NFS version."

The original design was identity-based: an opaque per-transport fairness key in svc_xprt (defaulting to source address with port excluded), a fixed hash-bucket scheduler guarded by a per-pool spinlock on the opt-in path only, an nfsd.fairq module parameter, and a clientid stamp for NFSv4.1 sessions. The result was a 72→182 ops/s recovery for the interactive client at K=8 with no aggregate throughput loss.

The review turned the design inside out

The discussion moved fast and in an unexpected direction. Three reviewers — NeilBrown, Chuck Lever, and Jeff Layton — converged on the same set of objections within a day:

NeilBrown's first pushback (June 3) rejected both the mechanism and the opt-in framing:

"I'm not keen on the hash bucket approach, though I can't clearly say why. I'm also not keen on the opt-in design. I don't like asking the admin to tune performance. We should always provide optimal performance."

He sketched a lockless alternative — a per-client object with its own lwq of xprts, itself in an lwq of clients, refcounted by xprts and RCU-freed — and argued that a per-client in-flight cap would only be acceptable "if the admin didn't have to tune it," noting v4.1 slot-based flow control could provide feedback but "With v3 I don't think there is any way to get the needed feed-back."

Daire Byrne (June 4) offered field context from a production NFSv3 deployment, describing workarounds they already run: svc_rpc_per_connection_limit, fq qdisc for per-stream maxrate, and prio qdiscs to prefer workstation subnets over a render farm. "I guess anything that can give us better control over these kinds of 'QOS' scenarios we'll happily take."

Chuck Lever's reframe (June 4) was the pivotal message. After agreeing with NeilBrown on dropping the module parameter ("a module parameter is outdated and way too global") and flagging identity as a trap — "fairness-by-client-address means all clients behind a NAT get reduced to a single fairness unit" — he proposed changing the problem class entirely:

"Instead, we could look at the problem as 'preventing starvation of any one connection' rather than the more difficult goal of 'ensuring complete scheduling fairness'. This changes the problem class fundamentally."

He argued that nothing in Coddington's data is technically starved today — the FIFO guarantees every backlogged transport one receive per cycle — but "the floor" degrades linearly with the aggressor's connection count. The reframed contract, he wrote, "dissolves the hardest challenges": no client identity, no NAT collapse, no client-object lifetime, and NFSv3/v4.0/LOCALIO all come for free. He pointed at fq_codel as prior art:

"fq_codel's success comes less from its fair queueing than from its sparse-flow optimization; that is, flows with nothing in flight jump the queue and heavy flows share what remains."

Coddington's synthesis: a burst-sized allowance

Coddington embraced the reframe and immediately identified its one real wrinkle. A single-RPC quantum only helps the first RPC of an interactive command — "loading a web page whose browser cache is on NFS is a readdir, then stat/open/read across many files." His rough numbers for a 50-RPC command against a 16-connection aggressor:

"today (per-transport FIFO): 50 * 17 / 650 ~= 1.3 s; sparse-flow, 1-RPC quantum: ~= 1.3 s (only the first RPC saved); whole command prioritized: 50 / 650 ~= 0.08 s"

His proposal: grant an idle→active connection a budget of ~N RPCs, re-granted each time it returns to idle. This stays identity-free and connection-count-proof because "a sustained stream never returns to idle, so it collects the allowance once and is in the bulk tier forever after." He flagged xpt_nr_rqsts as the existing in-flight signal needed for the sparse/bulk classification, committed to dropping the module parameter ("I'll drop the module parameter. With the reframe below the fair path is cheap enough to be always-on"), folding the measurement hook into the series, and producing new numbers with a properly interactive (not backlogged) client shape.

Lever agreed on prototyping: "Why not prototype a burst-sized allowance and then we can examine the behavior using your test harness to see how much of a difference it makes." He also pushed for a single mechanism over per-version fallbacks, keeping things "no more complicated than necessary."

Jeff Layton's late additions (June 4)

Layton's two messages closed out the week. On NeilBrown's v3 feedback question, he suggested:

"Shrink the TCP window to 0 for a bit on the connection(s)? That's the typical way to slow down a spammy client on a TCP socket. It's a bit coarse grained but typically the best we can do with v3. IDK, could we do something with the ECN flag?"

In his broader reply he endorsed the direction — "These seem like pretty clear improvements" — and offered practical guidance: IP address as default key with nfsd overriding later for sessionid/clientid; debugfs knobs acceptable during experimentation but "The goal at the end should be 'no tunables'"; per-session fairness for v4.1, clientid for v4.0, IP-without-port for v3. On the mechanism question he was agnostic: "Whatever performs best, most likely. This will be in a hot path."

Nature and significance

This is a new feature / scheduling redesign RFC, not a bug fix or protocol change — though it addresses what Coddington frames as a service-quality defect (linear degradation of the interactive floor with aggressor connection count). The week's outcome is a design pivot: from identity-based proportional fair queueing (hash buckets, clientid keys, opt-in module parameter) to an always-on, identity-free sparse-flow service floor keyed on xpt_nr_rqsts with a burst-sized priority allowance.

For NFS client developers, the practical implications are:

  • The reframe means NFSv3 and NFSv4.0 clients benefit without any protocol change or client-side cooperation — no new slot negotiation, no session identity required.
  • The "burst-sized allowance" design directly targets the interactive desktop / workstation use case (Daire's render-farm-vs-workstation scenario), which is where client developers most often hear about nfsd starvation complaints.
  • Coddington's commitment to include measurement/observability in the final series means client-side testing will have server-side instrumentation to correlate command-completion latency against aggressor connection counts.

Open items at week's end

  1. The burst-sized allowance (N) needs prototyping and measurement — "how many RPCs is an interactive cycle" is the one remaining parameter, with Coddington leaning toward a generous fixed default over an admin knob.
  2. NeilBrown's lockless per-client lwq design vs. the sparse/bulk tier approach are not yet reconciled; Coddington acknowledged the "hard part" of repointing xprts from v3-style (IP) to v4.1 (clientid) identity on session bind, suggesting a one-time XPRT flag.
  3. New benchmark numbers with a properly interactive client shape are owed.
  4. Jeff Layton's TCP-window-zero / ECN idea for v3 feedback is unexplored.
