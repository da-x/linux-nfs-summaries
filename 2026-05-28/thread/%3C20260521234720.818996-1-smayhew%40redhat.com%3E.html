rpcbind Memory Leak Fixes: Committed for 1.2.9-rc1

This Week's Activity

The thread reached its conclusion this week with a brief but definitive message from NFS maintainer Steve Dickson confirming that Scott Mayhew's three-patch series fixing memory leaks in the rpcbind daemon has been accepted and committed.

"Committed...(tag: rpcbind-1_2_9-rc1)" — Steve Dickson

Dickson's reply, posted on May 27, was characteristically terse — a single line confirming the merge with no additional commentary. The patches landed in the rpcbind 1.2.9-rc1 release cycle, signaling they were considered low-risk and appropriate for a stable release candidate.

Bridging Prior Week: The Patch Series

The original series was posted by Red Hat's Scott Mayhew on May 21 (the week prior), consisting of a cover letter and three surgical patches addressing leaks identified by valgrind:

"Fix a handful of leaks reported by valgrind." — Scott Mayhew, cover letter

The three patches targeted:

  1. init_transport() — freeing the transport address buffer after it had been consumed by the registration list, fixing a 19-byte leak per service registration.
  2. network_init() — two leaks: an unfreed getaddrinfo() result before reuse (128 bytes), and a missing freeifaddrs() call paired with getifaddrs() (1,296 bytes).
  3. read_warmstart() — freeing three string pointers within struct rpcb before freeing the parent struct rpcblist, fixing a 56-byte leak in the XDR decoding path during warmstart file reads.

The combined diff added just 10 lines across three files (src/rpcbind.c, src/util.c, src/warmstart.c).

Significance for NFS Clients and Servers

While rpcbind is user-space rather than kernel code, it sits squarely on the critical path for every NFS mount operation. The portmapper daemon must be running and responsive before any NFS client can resolve a server's RPC program numbers to network addresses. Memory leaks in a long-running daemon like rpcbind can degrade system performance over time, especially on heavily utilized NFS servers where services are frequently registered and unregistered.

These fixes, though small, improve the robustness of the entire NFS stack by ensuring that the portmapper does not silently consume memory across its lifetime — a meaningful quality-of-life improvement for administrators running long-lived NFS infrastructure.
