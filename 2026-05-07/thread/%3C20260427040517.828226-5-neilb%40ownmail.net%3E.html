VFS Locking Debates: Synthetic Inodes, iterate_shared(), and d_rehash() Retirement for AFS/CIFS

Summary

VFS/NFS: Locking Semantics, Synthetic Inodes, and d_rehash() Retirement Plans

Summary

Following last week’s resolution of a deadlock bug in __d_move() summarized previously, this week’s discussion between Al Viro and Neil Brown shifted to broader architectural concerns around directory iteration, synthetic inode numbers, and the long-term elimination of d_rehash() across network filesystems. The conversation represents an ongoing protocol/locking change effort rather than a discrete bug fix, focusing on how VFS APIs should constrain (or not constrain) filesystem-specific locking strategies.

Key Technical Arguments

1. Concerns About Locking Constraints for Filesystems with Synthetic Inodes

Al Viro pushed back against Neil’s characterization that transitional lock-dropping code would be "short-lived," warning of the risks of poorly defined constraints:

"... or we'll end up with hard-to-formulate constraints on what a filesystem may do with its internal locking to use the APIs provided by fs/{dcache,namei}.c safely." [id://20260501033715.GB3518998@ZenIV]

Viro highlighted that filesystems requiring synthetic inode numbers — where ->iterate_shared() needs to know what inumbers a concurrent stat(2) would inject into dcache — are "not uncommon." He contrasted procfs, which lacks directory-modifying operations like mkdir(), with more general cases:

"In procfs you are lucky to have no mkdir() and friends; the same is not true in general and we'd better have a sane answer to 'what could a filesystem like that do with its internal locking'. Or that thing will get blindly copied, with predictable results." [id://20260501033715.GB3518998@ZenIV]

2. Neil’s Counter: Synthetic Inodes Are Rare; Backing Stores Handle Locking

Neil Brown disputed the frequency of this pattern, noting he could only find it in procfs and overlayfs:

"You say 'not uncommon' but I can only find it in procfs and overlayfs." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

He argued that filesystems either use backing store inode numbers directly, populate via d_make_persistent(), or rely on the backing store’s own locking protocol:

"In either case, the backing store has its own locking protocol to manage create/remove/iterate, so the filesystem likely doesn't need to provide one." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

For procfs, Neil envisioned that once parent-locking is pushed down into the filesystem, all internal locking could be removed:

"For procfs I imagine that once we push parent parent-locking down in to the filesystem, we'll simply remove it all from procfs. Then there will be no problem calling d_alloc_parallel() in iterate_shared as the thread which owns the in-lookup dentry will never try to lock the parent." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

He characterized the current drop-and-retake lock pattern as purely transitional:

"The current code to drop and retake the lock is only transitional code to get from here to there in manageable steps." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

3. Proposed VFS-Level Helper for ->iterate_shared()

Viro floated a potential VFS enhancement to handle cases where d_alloc_trylock() fails during directory iteration:

"It might be worth teaching iterate_dir() that in such-and-such conditions it ought to save position, drop the lock, do a lookup on name stashed in dir_context, retake the lock and call back into ->iterate_shared() from saved position. With helper callable by ->iterate_shared() instances if they run into failing d_alloc_trylock() in a situation where they can't just shrug and move on... Not sure." [id://20260501033715.GB3518998@ZenIV]

Neil responded that the VFS would likely retain a shared lock on the parent during ->iterate_shared() to block rmdir while readdir is in progress:

"I think the VFS will still be taking a shared lock on the parent when calling ->iterate_shared. This is the easiest way to block rmdir while a readdir is happening." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

4. Eliminating d_rehash() from AFS and CIFS

In a significant side thread, Viro asked about Neil’s prior work removing d_rehash() usage from NFS in 2022, expressing interest in extending this cleanup to AFS and CIFS:

"BTW, do you have AFS and CIFS counterparts of your stuff from back in 2022 that killed d_rehash() uses in fs/nfs? I would love to kill d_rehash(); exfat use is an easily removable junk, but fs/afs and fs/smb/client ones are trickier and the reasons why it needed to be killed in fs/nfs apply to those as well." [id://20260501033715.GB3518998@ZenIV]

Neil confirmed that patches already exist for both filesystems, pointing to commits in his GitHub tree and corresponding lore.kernel.org threads from March 2026:

  • AFS: 20260312214330.3885211-20-neilb@ownmail.net and 20260312214330.3885211-21-neilb@ownmail.net
  • CIFS: 20260312214330.3885211-26-neilb@ownmail.net and 20260312214330.3885211-27-neilb@ownmail.net

Neil also invoked a decade-old CIFS commit, asking wryly:

"I don't suppose you have any memory of Commit: a00be0e ('cifs: don't use ->d_time') from 10 years ago. Maybe you didn't even see it." [id://177763238102.1474915.1506201584306941315@noble.neil.brown.name]

Final Conclusions

  • No immediate bug fix this week: The discussion was purely architectural, debating the long-term locking semantics for filesystems with synthetic inode numbers and directory-modifying operations.
  • Disagreement on prevalence: Viro views synthetic-inode filesystems as a common enough pattern to warrant careful API design; Neil considers them rare (procfs and overlayfs only) with manageable workarounds.
  • Transitional approach accepted: Neil’s drop-and-retake lock pattern is acknowledged as temporary, pending the push of parent-locking down into filesystem-specific inode_operations.
  • d_rehash() retirement on track: Patches to eliminate d_rehash() from AFS and CIFS already exist and are available for review, extending the 2022 NFS cleanup to other network filesystems — a significant step toward simplifying VFS dcache internals.

This discussion is about a protocol/locking change with implications for NFS client development, as well as broader VFS improvements that will benefit AFS and CIFS clients. The elimination of d_rehash() across multiple network filesystems represents an important cleanup effort that reduces complexity and potential race conditions in the dcache layer.
