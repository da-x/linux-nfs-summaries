svcrdma: Bounding Unbounded Send Context Cache to Prevent OOM

Summary

This week, the Linux NFS mailing list saw a significant discussion regarding an Out-Of-Memory (OOM) issue in the svcrdma subsystem, specifically related to the unbounded growth of the sc_send_ctxts cache under heavy RDMA load. The thread was initiated by Mike Snitzer and Benjamin Coddington from Hammerspace, who proposed a patch series to bound this cache and apply backpressure.

The Problem: Unbounded Cache Growth Leading to OOM

The issue was reported by a large customer site with 121 NFS clients and 9 NFS servers connected via RDMA. Under heavy NFS READ workloads (specifically 16K O_DIRECT reads), the NFS servers (each with 128GB RAM) would hit OOM conditions within ~10 seconds, with memory usage growing from ~12GB to exhaustion.

Benjamin Coddington diagnosed the root cause as an unbounded cache rather than a per-operation leak. The svc_rdma_send_ctxt_get() function pulls send contexts from a lockless list (llist) or allocates new ones if empty. However, svc_rdma_send_ctxt_release() always adds the context back to the list regardless of its size. The only cleanup occurs at transport teardown.

Two factors compounded this issue:

  1. Workqueue Lag: Context release is deferred to a workqueue (svcrdma_wq). Between the completion interrupt and the workqueue execution, contexts are "in transit."
  2. Cache Inflation: During this gap, concurrent requests see an empty list and allocate new contexts. When the in-transit context finally lands on the list, the cache grows by one. Under high HCA completion rates with even slight workqueue lag, this causes the cache to settle at the all-time peak of (in-flight + workqueue-pending) and never shrink.

Proposed Solution: Bounding the Cache

Patch 1/2 proposes tracking sc_send_ctxts_depth as the count of live contexts per transport. The cap is set to sc_max_requests, which represents the configured number of credit slots per transport. The patch applies backpressure in two places:

  • svc_rdma_send_ctxt_get(): If the list is empty and depth has reached sc_max_requests, it returns NULL, causing the caller to drop the connection. This forces the client to reconnect with a fresh transport, resetting the depth to zero.
  • svc_rdma_send_ctxt_release(): If depth exceeds the cap due to races or transient bursts, the context is freed instead of being returned to the list.

Mike Snitzer noted that while this approach avoids OOM kills, it substitutes one failure mode (OOM) with another (connection drops). He stated:

"Patch 1/2 is marked RFC because ultimately we suspect you'll have a better way to skin this cat... but Claude was pretty great at helping us cut through this nasty OOM situation with RDMA."

Diagnostic Tooling

A second patch provided a BPF diagnostic script (svcrdma-wq-lag.bt) to monitor workqueue lag. Mike Snitzer shared output from this tool, showing that before the cap was applied, there was a sustained deficit between wc_send rates (~432K/s) and release rates (~342K/s), leading to ~90K/s of contexts pinned as queued work items. With the patch applied, these rates matched within 1%, and no accumulation occurred over a 5-minute test.

Chuck Lever's Analysis and Alternative Approach

Chuck Lever, a key maintainer of the NFS server code, raised concerns about the proposed solution. He argued that the fundamental issue is why svcrdma_wq lags at steady state. He noted:

"The current svcrdma design assumes that the workqueue always keeps up with the transport's Reply transmission rate. For the send_ctxt cache to reach 2.3M ctxts, the workqueue itself must be running orders of magnitude slower than wc_send."

Chuck questioned whether the gap was structural or a result of slab pressure causing reclaim activity and subsequent workqueue starvation. He expressed concern that capping at sc_max_requests translates "workqueue is slow" into "drop the connection," which punishes the client for a server-side scheduling problem:

"Capping at sc_max_requests then translates 'workqueue is slow' into 'drop the connection.' That bounds memory but substitutes one failure mode for another, and it punishes the client for what is essentially a server-side scheduling problem. Not to mention the risks inherent in repeated spurious connection loss."

Chuck mentioned he had patches that replace the use of svcrdma_wq which could alleviate the spiral issue and offered them to Mike Snitzer for testing.

Conclusion and Next Steps

Mike Snitzer acknowledged that the imposed connection loss is not ideal but noted it was relatively rare in their tests, especially with larger I/O sizes where the workqueue could keep up. He agreed to test Chuck Lever's alternative patches:

"Yes, please share and we'll do our best to get time on the system."

The discussion highlights a critical bug fix area in NFS client/server development over RDMA, focusing on memory management and workqueue efficiency. While the proposed patch provides an immediate mitigation for OOM issues, the community is exploring more robust solutions that address the underlying workqueue lag without resorting to connection drops.
